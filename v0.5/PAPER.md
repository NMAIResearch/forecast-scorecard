# The AI Energy-Demand Forecast Scorecard: what data-centre electricity forecasts measure and what can be checked

Purpose: describe a source-traced revision of the AI Energy-Demand Forecast Scorecard that extends the IEA 4E review of data-centre energy estimates to 2025 and 2026 headline forecasts.

NM AI Research. Version 0.5, 17 October 2026. Independent claim-support verdicts for every registered observation are recorded in SUPPORT_REVIEW.json; the candidate observations carry current supported receipts in CANDIDATE_SUPPORT_REVIEW.json.

This work was produced through an AI-assisted workflow directed and reviewed by the author.

## Abstract

Forecasts of data-centre electricity demand from different publishers measure different quantities, draw different boundaries and date from different editions. Kamiya and Coroamă's critical review for the IEA 4E Technology Collaboration Programme catalogued over 100 publications by scope, time horizon and modelling approach and reported a much narrower range among the studies it rated higher in quality; Mytton and Ashtine traced the sources behind 258 published estimates. This revision of the AI Energy-Demand Forecast Scorecard extends that work to 2025 and 2026 headline forecasts from public bodies, research institutes, consultancies, banks and market analysts. Each registered figure is tied to a retained publication and an independent claim-support receipt, with dates, edition, target and base years, unit, quantity, scenario and coverage kept as separate fields. Candidate figures reach beyond the ledger's Global and US figures, to the Australian National Electricity Market, Europe and China as the IEA defines them, France, Denmark, Italy, Great Britain, Norway, Germany, Finland, Sweden, Belgium, Poland, Switzerland and the European Union. A revision is computed only where one publisher states both figures for the same quantity and both carry supported receipts, and every declined comparison is listed. Few of the ledger's figures come from publications the 4E review catalogues. Spans of stated figures are narrower within like-for-like groups than across all figures for the same metric and year, but the narrowing comes from separating geographies, and with few observations per group the sample cannot show whether comparable forecasts converge. The paper makes no demand forecast, accuracy ranking or capacity-uplift estimate.

## The question and why it matters

The scorecard asks two descriptive questions. What do selected 2025 and 2026 headline forecasts of data-centre electricity measure, and which comparisons between them can a reader support from the retained publications? And is the spread of their stated figures narrower within like-for-like groups, as the IEA 4E review found for the studies it rated higher in quality? It does not forecast electricity demand, rank forecast accuracy or estimate an uplift for announced capacity. Whether a difference between two published figures means anything depends on whether they measure the same quantity, on the same boundary, from the same base year; the sections below record what each registered figure measures, which comparisons the inspected evidence supports, and how far apart the stated figures remain once unlike quantities are separated.

## Earlier work

This version extends two reviews. Kamiya and Coroamă's critical review, prepared for the Efficient, Demand Flexible Networked Appliances platform of the IEA 4E Technology Collaboration Programme in March 2025 (P01 below), identified and reviewed over 100 articles, reports and statistics published since 2014. It catalogued each publication on affiliation, publication type, geographic scope, time horizon, modelling approach and analytical scope; recorded each study's values with its starting, ending and base years and one row per named scenario; removed cryptocurrency from the few assessments that included it; and, having found it challenging to define a rigid grading scheme, rated each study's quality by its two authors' independent judgement. Its recommendations ask modellers to define system boundaries and to avoid projections more than five years ahead, and ask journalists not to cherry-pick the most extreme scenario. Mytton and Ashtine (P02) traced 258 data-centre energy estimates from 46 publications through the 676 sources they used, argued that key sources must remain available so that calculations can be reproduced, and set out not to judge which estimate is more accurate.

Three publications from 2025 provide context. The guide by Koomey, Schmidt and Das for the Bipartisan Policy Center (P03) explains why regional load growth from data centres is hard to predict, including the overestimation that counting project proposals can cause; it states that the global estimates in its Figure C-1, which covers historical years, include only compute data centres and exclude network providers and cryptocurrency, compares projected growth rates with history, and judges projections of computing's electricity use more than a few years out to be no better than guesswork. London Economics International's report for the Southern Environmental Law Center (P04) evaluates projections of data-centre power demand growth by aggregating projections from US jurisdictions whose forecasts differ in issue date and periodicity; it interpolates years a forecast does not give at the cumulative average growth rate between two years, and extends a forecast whose outlook is too short by adding half of the change in demand between its last two years. The World Resources Institute (P05) asks why US forecasts differ so widely. Its explainer gives Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory's range of 325 to 580 TWh as a 2030 figure, while the 4E review records the same range from LBNL's December 2024 report as a 2028 figure (P01, section 3.2.1), which shows why the target year is kept as its own field.

For Europe, ENTSO-E's May 2026 report on data centres and the power system (P06) gives estimated data-centre electricity demand by European country for 2024, 2030 and 2035, with a minimum for 2030 and a minimum and maximum for 2035, and states that demand in Europe is expected to grow by more than 50% between 2025 and 2030, citing the IEA, the European Data Centre Association and an Accenture analysis of estimates from multiple sources. This version instead registers each figure from the forecasting body's own retained publication, with its own claim-support receipt.

What this version adds is narrower than either review. It applies the 4E distinctions to a small set of 2025 and 2026 headline forecasts, including capacity and growth measures, whereas the 4E review's metric is annual energy; binds each registered figure to a retained publication and an independent claim-support receipt; refuses rather than harmonises comparisons across quantities, boundaries, base years and editions; and lists every comparison it declines. It is an update and extension, not a new method for comparing forecasts. The searches for closer work behind this statement, run on 9 October 2026 and, for European compilations, on 11 October 2026, and the coverage search of 15 October 2026, were bounded and do not establish that none exists.

## Method

[RESULTS.json](RESULTS.json) records arithmetic, its denominators and the comparisons refused. A calculation runs only on observations whose current record carries a supported verdict from an independent reviewer; the Verification note describes how each verdict is bound to the records it covers. The drafting model's own assessment is recorded beside each observation and never enables a calculation. A declared comparison involving an observation short of full support is refused, and RESULTS.json records which comparisons ran.

The generated groups show recorded-value spans among supported observations sharing a geography, metric, unit, target year and base year. Finite extrema exclude lower-bound records, which are listed separately; observation and publisher counts describe all group members. An earlier vintage superseded by a later vintage of the same publisher series is listed separately rather than counted in the span, so a span does not mix a publisher's revision with differences between publishers.

Method descriptions are limited to wording located in the retained sources, including server-shipment modelling and project-pipeline scenarios. They are qualified descriptions, not measured quality scores. Unknown scope is retained as unknown. Same-publisher lineage cannot override an explicit change in scope, scenario, unit, target or baseline.

The comparison with the 4E review first establishes, from the review's tables of reviewed studies and its reference list, which of the ledger's publishers and editions the review catalogues, and then sets what the review records for each publisher beside what the ledger records. Values attributed to the review are transcribed as it records them; they are not re-verified against the publications it reviewed, and no value is harmonised, converted or compared as a revision across editions.

The spread comparison sets each like-for-like group beside two wider sets for the same metric, unit and target year: the same geography with any edition or base year, and any geography. Target years match as each source labels them, so in the any-geography set a financial-year target counts under its labelled year. All three sets use only observations with a current supported receipt; registered observations short of one are excluded and listed in the table, and candidates without one appear only in the candidates section. Each span reports the lowest and highest stated bounds, lists open lower bounds separately and leaves scenarios, shapes and boundaries as stated.

## Findings

### A common unit and target year are not sufficient

The refreshed evidence does not support treating a common unit and target year as sufficient comparability. Annual electricity, installed capacity, capacity demand and growth relative to a base year are distinct quantities. Cryptocurrency coverage, facility boundaries, scenario construction, publication vintage and the precision of the stated figure can differ even within a nominally similar group. The ledger retains those distinctions instead of converting them through unstated utilisation or baseline assumptions.

### Growth from a base year: Goldman Sachs

Goldman Sachs states its data-centre power demand outlook as growth from a base year and quantifies the underlying demand in gigawatts, so the ledger does not label the series as annual electricity. Its February 2025 statement is phrased as an upper end; the ledger records it as an upper bound with no central point, and it enters no calculation. The March 2026 summary names its immediately prior outlook value, and the September 2026 transcript states the March figure on a 2025 base. The ledger registers both as source-stated vintages (O19 and O20). Identifying O20 as a restatement of O11 on a different base is an inference. The transcript may differ from the audio, which was not checked. Each declared revision pairs figures on one base: the March revision against its named prior on the 2023 base (which the package refuses because both observations are short of full support), and the September figure against the March figure on the 2025 base. Comparing percentages across the two bases would assign a direction to quantities with different denominators, and the executable comparison refuses it. An implied change in the forecast level holds the underlying baseline estimate fixed and is conditional on that assumption.

### Installed capacity: BloombergNEF

BloombergNEF's later article names its December 2025 forecast as the comparator for the 2030 installed-capacity figure. For the 2035 figure the comparator is implied by date and the stated revision, and the December 2025 article words its figure as power demand. The current receipt for the December 2025 observation (O13) is partly supported because its installed-capacity classification is inferred. RESULTS.json therefore refuses the O13-to-O14 comparison (R03). A stated-point revision would require current supported receipts for both observations and would remain conditional on the inferred comparator. The records retain both the forecast-release date and the later article's publication date. Any such comparison would concern forecast change, not realised capacity growth.

### The figures added by the currency sweep

Six of the seven figures added by the currency sweep (O21 to O27, a small descriptive group) are recorded in gigawatts: McKinsey's global capacity demand and US IT demand, its global capacity for 2035, Gartner's power demand, S&P Global's US capacity and Deloitte's electricity demand, assigned to the US by inference; the seventh is Gartner's 2030 consumption in TWh. Capacity demand, IT demand and capacity of unstated type are recorded as distinct quantities and are not converted to annual energy. McKinsey's June 2026 page repeats the rounded 2030 global figure of its August 2025 article (O09 and O21) while citing a December 2025 article that is not retained; without established lineage the ledger records both as separate observations, with no supersession. Gartner states its 2030 consumption only as over 1,200 TWh; the ledger records that as an open lower bound (O27), which enters no comparison and is listed separately in value spans rather than treated as a maximum.

### A restated historical estimate: LBNL

LBNL's restated historical electricity estimate is kept outside the forward-forecast sample. Its later reference point lying within an earlier scenario range is an interval-containment observation. It does not establish an unchanged central forecast and is not automatically coded flat. Older numeric endpoints without fresh support remain in the preserved legacy ledger.

### Disclosed input links among providers

Several registered providers disclose input links. The IEA's data-centre model relies on a bottom-up approach developed by LBNL; Goldman Sachs states that it uses a 451 Research data set; Deloitte's November 2024 estimate cites Goldman Sachs and EPRI among its sources, which predates their later registered observations, and its April 2025 estimate names LBNL's 2024 report among its inputs. Agreement among linked providers therefore cannot automatically be counted as independent confirmation; the exact shared inputs and vintages are not established.

## Comparison limits

Annual electricity is energy used over a year, stated here in terawatt-hours; installed capacity, capacity demand and IT demand are power, stated in gigawatts; growth relative to a base year is a percentage whose meaning depends on the base. Converting one of these into another would need utilisation or baseline assumptions that the sources do not state, and the ledger supplies none.

Spans still combine scenario bounds with point estimates and differing cryptocurrency and facility boundaries. Their limits are not a probability interval, consensus estimate or validated like-for-like spread. Cryptocurrency appears among those boundaries because publishers draw the data-centre boundary differently: some publishers' data-centre figures include cryptocurrency mining, others exclude it and others leave it unstated, so a spread across them can mix boundaries. The ledger therefore records cryptocurrency coverage as one of the distinctions it retains rather than normalising it away.

## Limits

No metered US national data-centre outcome series was collected. The package cannot provide forecast-accuracy scores, a calibrated correction factor or a capacity-uplift instrument. It also does not prove that public access is complete or that a source is current indefinitely.

The historical discussion of misquotations, earlier demand concerns and utility forecast errors requires its own source review before renewed use. Those topics do not supply a measured error distribution for this sample. Historical overprediction elsewhere cannot establish that a current data-centre forecast is too high. The earlier paper remains preserved in its published record; this version does not recertify its remaining narrative claims.

The comparison with the 4E review relies on the review's own transcription of the publications it catalogued; those publications were not retrieved for this version, and the review's counts of scenarios and cases differ between its executive summary and its conclusions. The candidate receipts rest on TrendForce's press releases and article and on Oxford Economics' summary page and AEMO's explainer of their joint forecast; TrendForce's underlying report and the joint forecast's full report were not retained. The non-US candidates of version 0.4 rest on the IEA's 2026 report and 2025 data annex, the consumption chapter of RTE's Bilan prévisionnel 2025-2035 and the Danish Energy Agency's background note on data centres; RTE's 2030 values are read from a chart by the colour of their labels. Those added in version 0.5 rest on the publications named for them above: Statnett's and Elia's values are chart data labels, BloombergNEF's comes from a public article whose underlying report was not retained, and the German operators' and PSE's figures come from drafts of their plans. The non-US searches of 11, 15 and 17 October 2026 named the European Commission and ENTSO-E; RTE, EirGrid and Energinet; Terna and Snam, NESO, Statnett and NVE; and the German transmission system operators, Fingrid, Svenska kraftnät and Elia. On 17 October 2026 they also covered, in their own languages, the operators and public bodies of Poland, Ireland, Austria, Switzerland, Portugal, Belgium, Germany, France, Italy, Norway, Sweden and Finland, and those of the other countries of the European Union and the European Economic Area only through grouped English queries. An S&P Global page refused automated retrieval, and the Commission's report of September 2026 on data-centre energy efficiency was not found at its download address. Elia's study, saved in an ordinary browser, gives Belgium's 2030 figure as a chart label, which is registered. Regions outside Europe beyond China, Australia and the United States were not searched under these rules. Gartner's November 2025 press release, saved in an ordinary browser, states 448 TWh in 2025 rising to 980 TWh by 2030, the earlier ledger's value; that value, previously unresolved, is now listed as a known unregistered value (K09) with the release as its source. The bounded searches for publications dated 8 or 9 October 2026 and up to 15 October 2026 cannot exclude a publication that search indexes had not yet recorded, and values listed as known unregistered, including TrendForce's US figure, are not registered.

The reproduction checks described in the Verification note do not establish source truth or scientific validity. No forecast-quality or model-performance baseline was run.

## Reproducibility

The historical claim that an earlier script mapped open directly to reproducible is unverified in this bundle because that script is not included. That label did not establish an executed reconstruction of the forecasting model. The revision separates a readable publication, an inspected figure, a description of the method and an actual model-reproduction attempt.

This package recalculates the ledger and its declared comparisons from frozen transcriptions. No underlying provider model was reconstructed. Public tables can make outputs inspectable while commercial inputs, incomplete parameter records or unavailable execution code leave full model reproduction unresolved. An unattempted reconstruction is not evidence that reconstruction is impossible. ## Verification

The independent verdicts are recorded in SUPPORT_REVIEW.json. Each verdict is held as a receipt bound by digest to the observation and to the complete source catalogue, so a change to any evidence record withdraws the verdict. The candidate observations, added in versions 0.4 and 0.5, and their sources are recorded in CANDIDATE_OBSERVATIONS.json. A verdict on a candidate is held in the same way in CANDIDATE_SUPPORT_REVIEW.json, bound to the candidate record and to the complete candidate source list, so a candidate enters arithmetic only while a supported verdict binds its current record and sources. The earlier-work sources and the values attributed to the 4E review are recorded in PRIOR_WORK.json, with the SHA-256 of each retained original and printed-page locators.

Source references, precise locators and inspection limits are in SOURCE_CATALOGUE.json. Internal retained originals and separately hashed extractions support the source checks. Third-party originals are excluded from this distributable package. Native retrieval preflight was limited; readable originals were obtained separately where possible, and unresolved access remains explicit. For version 0.4, AEMO's overview refused an automated request and was retrieved with a request identified as a web browser; the other new sources were retrieved directly, except an S&P Global page that refused automated retrieval and is listed as a lead. For version 0.5, Fingrid's forecast, Svenska kraftnät's analysis, RTE's 2023 chapter and the Commission's second technical report were retrieved with a request identified as a web browser; Elia's study and Gartner's release of 17 November 2025 refused automated retrieval and were saved by the author in an ordinary browser; the other new sources were retrieved directly.

The reproduction command checks structured inputs, review-receipt digests, declared arithmetic, generated bytes and agreement with the supplied manifest. It also validates the candidate and prior-work records and the candidate receipts, and checks that the paper carries, verbatim, every table, count and statement generated from them and from the ledger. The tests cover refused comparisons across units, metrics, value bases, scenarios, scopes and base years; stale and short-of-supported review status; invalid quantities and years; duplicate keys; empty qualifications; open lower bounds, including a percentage-growth lower bound; agreement between the paper's generated statements and tables and the records; candidate admission only with a current supported receipt, and refusal of stale, short-of-supported, duplicate, malformed or unknown-candidate receipts; output corruption; and unsafe or partly written output destinations.

What the author cannot guarantee: a language model's output can be wrong in ways that survive review. Several methods have been deployed to mitigate this, including explicit instructions, internal red-teaming and cross-lab blindspot checks, but the author does not claim the review is exhaustive.
